Why is it hard to compare what coaches charge?

Coaching prices are hard to compare because they're built from different parts: a monthly fee, a minimum term, an up-front fee, sometimes events or extra seats, and different currencies. Some coaches publish a price and some give it on a call, so you often start without the figures side by side.

The monthly fee is the number people remember, and on its own it's the least useful one. A lower monthly fee with a twelve-month minimum commits you to far more than a higher fee you can leave after a month. The minimum term decides how much you lose if the coach turns out to be the wrong fit, and that's the figure most owners never work out before they sign.

The other cost is time. Count the calls, the travel to any events, and the work between sessions. Some of that time is work on your own business you'd want to do anyway. The rest is a cost like any other.

How to work out the real cost of a coach: the method, step by step

The method is to get five figures in writing from every coach on your list, turn them into two numbers, and compare those two numbers before you compare anything else.

  1. Ask every coach for the same five figures. The monthly fee and its currency, the minimum term, anything charged up front, what happens if you leave early, and anything that costs extra. Ask in writing, before the sales call, so the call can be about fit.

Copy and paste: the email asking a coach for the full cost

Hi [name],

Before we talk, could you send me the full cost in writing? I'm after the monthly fee and its currency, the minimum term, anything charged up front, what happens if I leave early (notice period and refunds), and anything that costs extra, such as events, travel or seats for my team.

I'm comparing a few options on year-one cost, so the figures help me come to the call ready.

Thanks, [your name]

  1. Turn the answers into two numbers. Year-one cost is what a full year costs you. Committed cost is what you've agreed to pay before you're able to leave. The second number is the one that tells you what you're risking.

Copy and paste: the year-one cost sheet

Coach: [name]

Monthly fee: $[amount], in [currency]

Minimum term: [months]

Up-front fee: $[amount]

Extras I'd actually use: $[amount]

Year-one cost: (monthly fee × 12) + up-front fee + extras = $[amount]

Committed before I can leave: (monthly fee × minimum term) + up-front fee = $[amount]

Hours a week it asks of me, calls and work between: [hours]

  1. Check the currency and the tax. A price in another currency moves with the exchange rate. Convert it at today's rate, and ask whether tax is included or added on top.
  2. Read the exit terms twice. Notice periods, refund rules and whether you can pause all change what leaving costs. If the answer to "what happens if I leave in month two" is vague, ask again until it isn't.
  3. Compare committed cost first, then fit. The lowest committed cost tells you which option risks the least. It doesn't tell you which coach can help. Once you have the figures, the companion guide on whether a coach is worth it turns year-one cost into a break-even bar per job.

A worked example

Here are two made-up offers in round numbers, not any real coach's prices, to show why the monthly fee alone misleads.

Offer A is $1,000 a month with a twelve-month minimum and $2,000 up front. Offer B is $1,500 a month, month to month, with nothing up front.

Offer AOffer B
Monthly fee$1,000$1,500
Minimum term12 monthsnone
Up-front fee$2,000$0
Year-one cost$14,000$18,000
Committed before you can leave$14,000$1,500

Offer A costs less over a full year. If it turns out to be the wrong room, Offer A has cost you $14,000 and Offer B has cost you $1,500. Neither number says which coach is better.

The mistakes owners make when comparing coaching prices

Four mistakes make one coach look cheaper or dearer than it is.

  • Comparing monthly fees only. The minimum term and the up-front fee can outweigh the difference in monthly price.
  • Booking the sales call before you have the figures. Ask in writing first. A call spent finding out the price is a call not spent on your business.
  • Forgetting the exit. Find out what leaving costs before joining, while it's an easy question to ask.
  • Treating the cheapest option as the safest. The safest option is the one with the lowest committed cost that can also do the job.

The rule of thumb for weighing coaching costs

Compare coaches on what you're committed to before you can leave, then on fit, and only then on the monthly fee. Benchmark: five figures in writing from every coach (monthly fee, minimum term, up-front fee, exit terms, extras), turned into year-one cost and committed cost.
