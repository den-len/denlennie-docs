Video business coach vs a generic business coach

Hire a specialist video business coach when most of your problems come from how video gets quoted, made and delivered: quotes that leak margin, revision rounds that eat profit, a business that stops when you stop shooting. A strong generalist can serve you well when the problem is one any small business has. Either way, sort your problems first and test the coach on your industry before you pay.

Why does the specialist or generalist choice matter for a video business?

The choice matters because a video production company has failure modes a generalist has to learn before they can coach them, and you pay for that learning time.

Project work brings lumpy cash flow. Scope creeps through revision rounds nobody defined. Pre-production gets given away because nobody put it on the quote. The owner books themselves onto every shoot because nobody else can be trusted with the camera, and hides in the edit because it feels safer than selling. Clients don't care about gear, codecs or creative vision. They care about results, budget and timelines. A coach who has run a production company has seen all of this from the inside.

That doesn't make every generalist the wrong choice. Plenty of problems aren't specific to video at all: running a team meeting that ends with decisions, reading a profit and loss statement, holding a difficult conversation with a staff member. A good generalist with a strong record on those can be the right hire. The question is which kind of problem you'd be bringing.

How to decide between a specialist and a generalist: the method, step by step

The method is to write down your problems, sort each one into industry or general, then test any coach you're considering against the industry ones.

  1. List the three problems you'd bring to the first call. One sentence each, in your own words. If you can only write one, that's fine.
  2. Sort each problem. Run each one through a single test question, below. Mark it industry or general.

Copy and paste: the sorting test

Problem: [one sentence]

Would a dental practice or an accounting firm have this exact problem? [yes or no]

Does the answer depend on how video gets quoted, shot, edited, revised or delivered? [yes or no]

If the first answer is yes and the second is no, mark it general. Otherwise, mark it industry.

  1. Count them. If most of your list is general, a strong generalist is a reasonable choice. If most of it is industry, you want someone who has run a production company.
  2. Test the coach on your industry. Ask the questions below on the first call, or in writing. You're listening for specifics: a specialist answers in terms of quotes, rounds, crews and clients. Anyone who answers "business is business" has told you what you need to know.

Copy and paste: five questions to test a coach on video production

1. Have you run a video production company yourself, and for how long?

2. Walk me through how you'd structure the quote for a two-day corporate shoot with three edits.

3. What do you tell an owner whose client keeps asking for another round of changes?

4. I'm on every shoot. What would you have me hand over first, and to whom?

5. How many video production owners have you worked with, and what happened to their numbers?

  1. Check the answers against what good looks like. The table below is a guide to what a specialist's answer usually covers. A generalist may answer some of these well too, which is useful to know.
QuestionA specialist's answer usually covers
The two-day shoot quoteSeparate sections for pre-production, production and post, a contingency line, and a production company fee on top
Another round of changesDefining a round on the quote, pricing the extra round before round one starts, counting rounds at every delivery
Handing over the shootWhich role to hand over first, what gets written down before it's handed over, and how you check the work
Their numbersA figure with a method behind it, across a stated number of owners, that you can check

A worked example

Here's a made-up owner in round numbers, to show the sort in practice.

The owner runs a production company at about $400,000 a year and writes down three problems. Quotes keep going out without a production company fee. The weekly team meeting runs for two hours and decides nothing. Every shoot still needs the owner behind the camera.

ProblemSorting testMark
No production company fee on quotesDepends on how video gets quotedIndustry
The team meeting decides nothingA dental practice could have this exact problemGeneral
The owner is on every shootDepends on how video gets shot and who's trusted to shoot itIndustry

Two of the three are industry problems, so this owner shortlists coaches who have run a production company, and asks each one the five questions. If the list had come out two general and one industry, a strong generalist would have been a fair choice, with the industry problem tested separately.

The mistakes owners make when choosing between the two

Four mistakes send owners to the wrong kind of coach.

  • Choosing on proximity. A coach a friend recommends, or one who's local, still needs to pass the sorting test and the five questions.
  • Assuming a specialist covers everything. Specialists have gaps too. Ask what they don't cover, and listen for a clear answer.
  • Hiring the coach whose answer you liked most. Agreement isn't diagnosis. The coach worth paying is the one who tells you something you didn't want to hear and can show why.
  • Skipping the sort. Owners often bring the problem they enjoy working on, not the one costing them money. Sort the whole list before you choose.

The rule of thumb for specialist vs generalist

If most of the problems you'd bring to the first call depend on how video gets quoted, made and delivered, hire someone who has run a video production company; if most don't, a strong generalist will do. Benchmark: three problems sorted with the test question, and five industry questions put to every coach on the shortlist.
