How to give clients a reason to book now, without discounting

Clients delay video work because nothing forces a decision, so give them a specific, true reason to act this month: something extra they get by booking, a date that really is closing, or a smaller, safer first step. Put the extra on the quote at its real price and mark it as included, so your production company fee is calculated on the full job. Start with past clients, test three offers for a month, and keep the ones that book.

Why do interested clients keep putting off the booking?

Interested clients put off booking because a lot of video work has no deadline they can feel. The brand film can wait until next quarter, the testimonial series until the new website is live, and every week of waiting costs them nothing.

So the conversation goes quiet. The client hasn't said no. "Let's pick this up in the new year" is simply the easiest thing to say, and your follow-ups get polite replies and no dates.

The way I put it on our calls: "There should be an offer made and there should be a deadline for acting." An offer is that reason, written down, with a date on it.

Why is a discount the wrong offer?

A discount is the wrong offer because it lowers what the client expects to pay you next time, and it comes straight out of your margin.

The rule I come back to with members is short: never drop your price, drop your scope. If the budget is tight, take something out of the job. If you want the client to move now, add something to it. Your rates and your production company fee stay where they are.

One member used to give away captions, transcripts and window dubs. None of it was on the quote, so the client never saw it as part of what they were buying. Open captions are now an agreed extra on his jobs, priced like everything else.

How to build an offer that gets a booking: the method, step by step

The method is six steps: start with people who already know you, pick one kind of reason, price the extra and include it, give it a date, put it in the proposal, and hold your rates when the client hesitates.

  1. Start with people who already know you. Past clients and warm leads trust you already, so a small reason is enough to move them. One member built a case study and sent it to clients he hadn't spoken to in a long time. A client from a 2024 shoot came back with a product launch and booked a shoot. You're ready for the next step when you have a list of everyone you've worked with in the last two years.
  1. Pick one kind of reason. There are three, and each offer uses one:
    • Something extra: more from the shoot they're already paying for (vertical cutdowns for social, captioned versions, a library of stills, team headshots while the lights are up).
    • A date that's really closing: your quiet-month shoot dates, a rate rise you've actually scheduled, a launch or event date that needs lead time, or the end of their budget year if their budget lapses (ask, don't assume).
    • A smaller first step: a paid pilot credited in full against the bigger job, or a paid creative development fee credited if they go ahead.
  1. Price the extra, then include it. Put the value-add on the quote as a priced post-production line, at its real cost. Calculate the production company fee on the production cost (subtotal plus contingency) with the value-add in it, then show the value-add as included. The client sees what they're getting for nothing. Any extra edit carries the same revision rounds as the main job, and the quote says so.

Copy and paste: the included value-add, as a quote line

Three vertical cutdowns for social (9:16), cut from the main shoot, with [n] rounds of revisions each, the same as the main film. Value $[price]. Included when the project is confirmed by [date].

  1. Give it a date the client can check. Use a real one: when your quiet-month dates go, when your new rates start, when the launch needs the film. If you can't say why the date is the date, leave it out. This step is done when the offer line on the quote has a date in it.
  1. Put it in the proposal. An offer works best inside the quote, either as a priced optional line or as the difference between options. With three options on the page, the client chooses between configurations.
  1. Hold your rates. If the client says it's too much, offer a smaller scope at the same rate, then give them time. One member quoted £1,200 for half a day plus the edit to film a DJ's set. The reply was "too much". He left it, and three or four days later the DJ came back.

Copy and paste: the past-client email

Hi [first name],

I was looking back at the [project] we made together and there's a lot of good footage we never used. I could cut [three short vertical clips / a 60-second version / an updated version with this year's figures] from it for [price], ready in [timeframe].

I've got edit time free in [month], so if you'd like them for [their next campaign or event], let me know by [date] and I'll book it in.

[Your name]

Copy and paste: the quiet-dates email

Hi [first name],

I've got [number] shoot days still open in [month]. They usually go to clients who've worked with us before, so I'm letting you know first.

If [the project you mentioned] is still on the list, booking one of those dates by [date] means you'd also get [value-add] included, at no extra cost.

Want me to hold one for you?

[Your name]

A worked example

Here's what options inside a proposal can do, from a member in the UK. A regular client of two years wanted a three-day shoot across two cities. He asked for their budget before proposing, and they said £3,000 "with some wiggle room", plus travel and accommodation.

He pointed out that past jobs had been one-day shoots and this one was three, then sent a one-page proposal with three options, built from a template another member had shared in the community. He expected them to pick the middle one. They took the top option, which he puts at between five and seven thousand pounds. In his words: "There was no pushback at all."

The mistakes that undo it

Most offers fail for one of four reasons.

  • Discounting the fee. The production company fee pays for running the job well. The one exception is a deliberate, openly stated anchor-client rate of 12 percent or above, given in exchange for committed work.
  • Giving extras away unpriced. If the value-add isn't on the quote at a price, the client can't see it and you can't count its cost.
  • Inventing a deadline. "Offer ends Friday" with nothing behind it teaches clients your dates don't mean anything.
  • Paying for referrals or reviews upfront. Give the referral thank-you only after the introduction has booked. And don't offer anything in exchange for a Google review: Google's review policy doesn't allow incentivised reviews. A written testimonial for your own website is a fair trade.

The rule of thumb for offers

Every offer either adds something to the job or sets a real date, and none of them lowers your rates. Benchmark: three offers, tested for one month, sent to past clients first, with every value-add priced on the quote and shown as included.

Hold yourself accountable

Which of these have you taken on or put in place recently?

  • Your quote template has a priced value-add line you can mark as included.
  • You've sent an offer with a date to at least five past clients this quarter.
  • Your last proposal gave the client options rather than one price.
  • The last time a client said "too much", you offered a smaller scope at the same rate.
