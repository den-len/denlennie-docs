VBA · Strategic PlanningSTEP 03

Organise your intentions.

This is not business planning. Business planning is a document you write once and file. This is the work of deciding what you are actually for, what you are aiming at, and what you will do about it in the next ninety days. Do it properly and it changes how you make every decision for the next twelve months.

First time through, this is your annual plan and you look back across twelve months. If you have done this before, it is your quarterly plan and you look back across the last quarter only.

Before you start

Some of this will feel unusual. You might not have been asked these questions before, and the first few pages have nothing to do with marketing, sales or shoots. Sit with that. This process has been run across hundreds of video business owners and the discomfort is part of it.

There is an old line worth keeping in mind today: life is pain, suffering is optional. Most of what makes running a business feel like suffering is not the work. It is the absence of a plan, which turns every decision into a fresh negotiation with yourself. Structure will set you free. That is the whole argument for the next ninety minutes.

One rule for today. Don't judge, just write. In the early exercises you are collecting raw material, not editing it. The editing comes later, and it comes fast. If you stop to assess every item as it lands, you will write six things instead of sixty and your plan will be built on the wrong six.

What we're doing, in order

  • Where you are. Your stage, your gate criteria, your systems, your energy. Scores, not essays.
  • Your number. Revenue, EBIT, and what you're willing to trade for them.
  • Your values. The ones you actually operate by.
  • Looking back. What you achieved, and what it taught you.
  • The chain. Turning that into objectives with dates on them.
  • Your quarterly TOPs. The three to five things that matter for the next ninety days.

You won't finish everything today, and you're not meant to. Get your number, three rows of the chain worked properly, and three TOPs you believe. Finish the rest this week while it's warm.

Find your stage before you plan anything

Every video business hits the same wall, and which wall you're at decides what your plan should contain. Plans fail most often not because the goals were wrong but because they were the right goals for a stage the owner hadn't reached yet. Systems built on chaos collapse. Simplify first, systemise second.

Read all five. Tick the one that describes your business as it actually runs today, not the one you're nearly at.

Be honest, then be honest again. Most owners place themselves one stage higher than they are. The gate criteria below are the reality check. If the gates for the stage you picked aren't ticked, you're working at the stage below and your plan should say so. Tick a stage above to see its gates.

Your gate criteria

These are the things that must be true before the next stage is worth attempting. Tick what's genuinely done. What's left unticked is where your annual priorities come from, so this page does most of the work in the next hour.

Stage 1 · OperatorActive pillar: Survival

The move forward isn't speed, it's focus. One clear offer, one defined type of client, one repeatable way work comes in.

Define the niche and set the minimum. Everything else at this stage is noise until those two are locked. Write what that means for you, specifically.

Stage 2 · JugglerActive pillar: Control

The constraint isn't process, it's decision authority. Your team waits for you because you haven't defined what they own.

Write the delivery process down and create one case study. Those two shift you from reactive to repeatable and give you something to show prospects. What does that mean for you?

Stage 3 · StabiliserActive pillar: Alignment

You're still the glue. The business works because you hold it together, and that makes growth fragile.

Get off the editing. That single move frees the time to do everything else at this stage. Until it happens there's no slack to build anything. What's your first step towards it?

Stage 4 · StrategistActive pillar: Leverage

Leadership is the constraint now. Not systems, not team, not demand. The risk is optimising when you should be redesigning.

Raise the price floor and set up a weekly leadership rhythm. Together they force the shift from manager to leader. What's the number, and when's the meeting?

Stage 5 · ScalerActive pillar: Expansion

No gate here. This stage is calibration. Scale isn't the challenge, focus is, and every new opportunity carries an opportunity cost.

What should I not do next? That's the constraint at this stage. Not what to add, what to protect. Answer it here.

Time with family? Health? Lifestyle? Energy? The bigger the number, the bigger the trade. Name it now, because you'll pay it either way.

The line. Write it down while you're calm, because the moment you'll need it is the moment a big project is dangling in front of you.

The whole game, in one line

Unless you leverage, more money just means more of you.

External support, team, contractors. Without one of those, a bigger number means more time spent doing the work yourself, and there's a hard limit on that. You'll hit a ceiling, or you'll burn out, and most people manage both.

This is the Operator's trap, and it's why the staircase exists. Effort is a growth strategy right up until it isn't. If your number needs more hours than you have, the number isn't the problem and neither are you. The structure is.

Against your number, name the leverage. If the honest answer is "no one, it has to be me", write that down and go back to your stage. That answer is the diagnosis.

Values: what you'll hire and fire on

Worth doing even if you're a one-person business, because at some point you'll bring someone in, and a defined set of values makes that selection obvious rather than agonising. Once you have a team, build the final set with them so they're connected to it.

Push past integrity, honesty and quality. Everyone writes those and nobody operates by them. For each value you write down, think of a moment in the last year where holding to it cost you money or comfort. If you can't find one, it isn't a value yet, it's a preference.

Step one: look back

Before you decide what's next, you need the raw material of what's happened. Write everything you think of in each area. Don't judge, just write. Most people find the list gets longer as they go, and things they'd forgotten surface halfway down. That's the exercise working.

What you've achieved, so you can see it properly. Owners are chronically bad at this and it skews every judgement that follows. Be generous with yourself here.

What you've learned, or found out you need to learn. The good, the bad and the moments the penny dropped.

Step two: the chain

This is the part that makes the difference, and it's the part most planning misses. What you've just written is raw material. It isn't a plan and it never becomes one by being read again. It becomes a plan by travelling.

Every item that matters moves left to right across the same row, and it changes shape as it goes. A thing that happened becomes a thing you learned, becomes a question, becomes an obstacle named out loud, becomes an objective, becomes a date. One row. Nothing gets stranded on a page you never turn back to.

01
Accomplishment

What happened. Good or bad, it's evidence.

02
Lesson learned

What it taught you. This is where most people stop.

03
How do we...

The outcome you want, phrased as a question.

04
Given that...

The obstacle, named. Don't solve it yet.

05
Objective

Written as though it's already true.

06
Pillar and date

Which pillar it serves, and when it's done.

Not every row starts at the left. Some strategic issues don't come from an accomplishment at all, they come from a gate criterion you couldn't tick or a system you scored 3 out of 10. Those rows start at column three. That's fine. The columns are a shape, not a queue.

The pillar column, and why it's there

Column six is the one that isn't in the old version, and it's the one that stops this becoming a wish list. Every objective gets tagged with the pillar it serves: Survival, Control, Alignment, Leverage or Expansion.

Then the honest bit. If an objective belongs to a pillar above your stage, it doesn't go in this year's plan. It goes in the parking lot at the end. Not because it's a bad idea, but because it's the right idea at the wrong time, and doing it now costs you the thing you actually needed to do. The instinct to jump is usually right about the direction and wrong about the timing.

A worked row: "Landed the Emerald Grain job" (accomplishment) → "Agriculture pays better and briefs cleaner than events" (lesson) → "How do we get more agriculture clients" (how do we) → "Given that we have no case study and no list" (given that) → "We have two agriculture case studies published and a named list of 50 agriculture prospects in the CRM" (objective) → "C, end of Q3" (pillar and date).

Column five, where plans live or die

A complaint can't be ticked, measured or delegated.

  • The problem: not enough website enquiries.
  • The outcome: want more leads. Still useless. That's a feeling, not a target.
  • The objective: "We have a fully functional strategy session funnel that is automated, delivering five qualified leads per week."

Present tense, as though it's already true. That isn't a trick. Writing it as done forces you to describe the finished thing, and the finished thing is what you work backwards from. Every objective in column five starts with "We have".

Your chain

Work across, not down. If a row runs out of steam by column four, it wasn't a strategic issue. Today, do three rows. Three worked properly beats twenty half-done. Finish the rest this week while it's warm.

Step four: quarterly TOPs

TOPs are your Tactical Operating Priorities. Pull three to five from the annual list, per person or per area, and then ignore the annual plan completely for the next ninety days. That's the discipline that makes this work. The annual list exists so you're not choosing blind. It doesn't exist to be looked at daily.

Be realistic. If your annual priority is 100 leads a month and you're at 40, your quarterly TOP is 50 a month, not 100. Bite-sized and measurable, reviewed every quarter.

Quarterly TOP Who When

One line. It should sound like your domino move from earlier, because it usually is.

The parking lot

Every objective you tagged with a pillar above your stage goes here. This page is not a bin. It's the reason the rest of the plan is achievable, and next year most of this becomes the plan.

For each one, write the gate criterion that unlocks it. "Hire a second editor, parked until editing is delegated and the buy-back rate is holding." That's not a no. That's a not yet with a condition on it, which is the only kind of not yet that ever turns into a yes.

The rhythm in the room

The last piece isn't on your calendar, it's in the community, and it's the cheapest accountability you will ever get.

Sunday

Post your weekly goals

Set your intentions for the week ahead. Three strategic goals, posted in Skool where the room can see them.

The discipline is in the word strategic. Your weekly goals are a subset of your quarterly TOPs, not a to-do list of busy tasks. If it isn't traceable back to a TOP, it doesn't go in the post.

Friday

Post your weekly wins

Celebrate yours and other people's. There are always wins, including in the weeks that felt like a write-off, and especially in those.

This isn't a morale exercise. Owners are chronically bad at noticing progress, and a year of unnoticed progress is how people talk themselves out of a business that's actually working.

Why it works

If you post, you get support.

A plan nobody knows about is a wish. Post it and the room does three things to it: someone tells you the thing you're avoiding, someone offers the shortcut they already paid for, and someone asks you on Friday whether you did it.

That last one is worth more than any of the paperwork in this workbook.

One thing, with a day on it. Then go and post it.

The standards that make the room worth posting in are in the VBA Code of Conduct, which every member signs up to: extreme ownership, ego at the door, monthly financial review, and the standing permission to be called out on a story you're telling yourself. If you post a plan you don't believe, someone will say so. That's the feature.

Before you log off

Three things.

  • Your number, with the EBIT behind it and the trade named honestly
  • Three rows of the chain, worked all the way across to a pillar and a date
  • Three quarterly TOPs, each with a who and a when

Three. Not the whole workbook. If you log off with a number you've stress-tested and three priorities you believe, the session did its job.

Then finish it this week, while it's still warm. The rest of the chain, and the objectives that came out of it. Give it an hour before the weekend and you'll have a year's plan instead of a good afternoon.

Then post your TOPs where the room can see them. A plan nobody knows about is a wish.

And when it starts to feel heavy, which it will: this is simple, this is easy, this is fun. Say it out loud. Den has it on a label on his monitor next to a second one that reads "is this a priority right now?". Both earn their space.
