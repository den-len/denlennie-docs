Content or outreach: where to spend your marketing time

Many of the owners I coach over-engineer their content and under-execute their outreach: hours on the bio and the posting plan, minutes on contacting people who could hire them. If you spent more hours on content this week than the number of people you contacted, outreach is your problem. Pick one channel, run a seven-day outreach test, and judge it on the conversations it starts.

Download this guide as a PDF

Why do video business owners spend hours on content and minutes on outreach?

Video business owners spend hours on content because polishing a profile feels productive and safe, while contacting someone who could hire you feels exposing. The safe work is easier to start, so it gets the hours.

I see it across the owners I coach. One spent hours on an Instagram bio. Another went back and forth on a LinkedIn page strategy. A third kept debating between LinkedIn connection approaches instead of trying one. The coaching was the same every time: pick one, execute, adjust from the data.

The polishing looks like work, which is why the cost is easy to miss. The coaching put it plainly: the seven followers lost mean nothing, and the diagnostic follow-up email matters.

There's a second reason content wins the week. Most people reach out to a prospect once, a few follow up, and most give up and go looking for something new. One message with no reply feels like evidence that outreach doesn't work, and the owner goes back to the bio.

How do you tell whether content or outreach is your actual problem?

You tell by putting two numbers side by side: the hours you spent on content this week, and the number of people who could hire you that you contacted. If the hours are bigger, outreach is the problem. The five questions below sharpen the answer.

Copy and paste: the content or outreach self-check

1. In the last seven days, how many hours did I spend on content (profile, bio, website, posting plan), and how many people who could hire me did I contact? If the hours are bigger: outreach is the problem.

2. Is there a follow-up I know I should send and haven't? If yes: outreach is the problem, and that message goes first.

3. The last time I changed channel or approach, had the old one run long enough to give me data, or did it just feel slow? If it just felt slow: the problem is execution. Go back to one channel and test it properly.

4. Can I list ten people I'd like to work with, where they spend their time, and what in their situation is putting them at risk right now? If not: my outreach has no target. Build the list before posting anything else.

5. When someone I contact looks me up, does my profile say who I help and show one piece of proof? If not, and I'm already contacting people: that's the one content fix worth making. Otherwise leave the profile as it is.

How to run a seven-day outreach test: the method, step by step

A seven-day outreach test is one channel, worked every day for a week, logged, then reviewed. It's adapted from the seven-day test I run in workshops, where the week mixed a posting plan with connecting and then we looked at the results. This version is outreach only, and it keeps two lines from the workshop slide: what gets measured gets managed, and do it five times a day.

  1. Write your message in one line. Use the equation: We help [niche] to [get result] by [offer]. If you can't say it clearly, your target market will be confused and your marketing won't work. In the workshop deck this line went into the connection message. In this version it stays with you as the line you check every message against, and the message itself leads with their situation.
  2. Build a short list. Answer four questions about the people you want to work with: who are they, where are they, what is their pain, and what is driving their decision. Prospects don't change the status quo because of their job title. They move when their current situation is putting them at risk, and a decision maker won't do anything that puts their own job at risk. Look for the people whose situation is already making the case, and list enough names for five contacts a day.
  3. Pick one channel, where those people already are. LinkedIn or email, whichever your list actually uses. On LinkedIn, reach comes from commenting thoughtfully in the right rooms, where your kind of client is already talking, rather than from sending more connection requests. Check the host's audience is your client before you comment.
  4. Book the time seven days ahead. Put one block in the diary for each of the seven days before you start. Done looks like seven blocks in the calendar before day one.
  5. Make five contacts a day. Five is the number for this test, taken from the workshop's five a day. A contact is a thoughtful comment in a room where your client talks (including on a post by someone on your list), or a short personal message with one question in it. Give first.
  6. Log every contact. Name, date, what you sent, whether they replied, and the next step. The workshop's four steps were connect, content, nurture, track, and this is the track.
  7. Review on day eight. The next section shows how to read what you've got.

Copy and paste: a first message to someone you'd like to work with (a template to adapt)

Hi [name],

I saw [something specific: a post they wrote, a project they launched]. [One sentence on what you thought of it.]

I run a video production company that works with [type of business].

How are you handling [the specific problem] at the moment?

[Your name]

Copy and paste: the seven-day test plan

Channel (one only): [LinkedIn or email]

My line: We help [niche] to [get result] by [offer]

The list: [names of people I'd like to work with, enough for five a day]

Diary: [time] every day for seven days, booked before day one

Every day: five contacts. A thoughtful comment in a room where my clients talk (including on a post by someone on my list), or a short personal message with one question.

Log for each contact: name / date / what I sent / replied (yes or no) / next step

Day eight review: contacts made [ ] of 35. Replies [ ]. Conversations started [ ]. Follow-ups due [ ].

Decision: keep the channel / change the message / change the list

How do you read the result of a seven-day test?

Read a seven-day test on one number: conversations started with people who could hire you. Followers, likes and views are vanity numbers. Seven days tells you whether you did the work and whether your message starts conversations.

A widely used sales rule of thumb, one I've used in workshops, explains why the week can't tell you much more. For every 100 people you make contact with, about 60 aren't interested, 30 are interested but not right now, 7 are open to buying and 3 are ready to buy now. It describes readiness to buy, not how many people will reply.

Three readings I'd suggest for day eight:

  • You didn't make the 35 contacts. The channel hasn't been tested yet. Run the same seven days again before changing anything.
  • You made the contacts and almost nobody replied. Try changing the message or the list before the channel. Go back to steps one and two.
  • You got replies. Keep the channel. Have the conversations, put everyone who said "not right now" on a follow-up list with a date, and run another seven days.

The mistakes that undo it

The seven-day test fails in three predictable ways.

  • Switching channels on a feeling. Decide the test length before you start and let it finish, even if the first three days are quiet. The coaching on paid ads was the same: they need to run longer before you kill them.
  • Skipping the log. Without names, dates and replies written down, the day-eight review is a guess about how the week felt.
  • Pitching in the first message. The workshop deck put a case study in the first message after connecting. I'd hold it back now: the first message asks one question about their situation, and the case study and the call come once they've replied.

The rule of thumb for content and outreach

Contact people before you polish anything: one channel, five contacts a day, logged, and reviewed every seven days.

The benchmark is 35 logged contacts a week, with everyone who said "not right now" on a follow-up list with a date.

Hold yourself accountable

Which of these have you taken on or put in place recently?

  • You know how many hours you spent on content last week and how many people you contacted.
  • You've sent the follow-up you'd been putting off.
  • You've run one channel for seven days at five contacts a day and logged every one.
  • You've reviewed the week on conversations started and decided: keep the channel, change the message, or change the list.
